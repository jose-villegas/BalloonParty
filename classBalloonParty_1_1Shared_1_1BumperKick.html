Detailed Description

The forgiving kick's geometry: a ball leaving a cap close enough to the line of a cap of another wall is bent onto it, and landed where its next bounce carries the rally on.

ForgivingKickRule asks it for the game, the shot solver and the aim line.

The target. From the contact point the kick takes the mirror reflection, then looks at every cap of another wall (BumperCircle.Wall) whose bearing lies within the cone of it and whose corridor is clear. The one at the smallest angle wins, the lowest id on a tie; with none the ball leaves along the reflection. A cap of the wall just hit is never a target: inside one wall every bounce is the mirror reflection, so a wall cannot hand the ball along itself.

The landing point looks one cap ahead. The ball is aimed at the point of the target from which a mirror reflection sends it on toward a follower. The ball then leaves the target along a reflection that already points at the follower, so the target's own kick picks the follower and repeats the step: the rally carries itself. The offset that lands the ball there (BumperKickTuning) is kept within the tuning's two offsets: never the centre, never a thin graze.

The follower is the nearest cap of another wall. Every cap that is not of the target's own wall is tried, the nearest to the target first (the lower id on a tie; where a cap stands in the list does not matter). Two walls that face each other are each other's nearest, so the ball is sent back across the gap between them, which is how two walls give three hits. A follower fits when the offset it needs is within the tuning's two, the reflection's way to it is clear, and the kicked line that lands the ball for it has a clear corridor. When none fits, the ball is landed for the nearest of them as near as the offsets allow; when that line is blocked too, at the smallest offset, as the last kick is. A contact whose cap is not in the list is not kicked.

The last kick. The bounce that brings the count to the chain target is aimed at the hit that banks, whose own bounce is not kicked. With no chain left to set up it lands at the smallest offset: a reflection aimed at a follower's centre would be a dead-centre hit with no kick left to correct it. The side is the one the reflection leaned to, or the other when that line is blocked. A reflection that points straight at the centre leans to neither; the side the hit mushroom's surface faces decides then.

The corridor is the path of the ball's centre along the kicked line, from the contact point to where it meets the target. It is blocked when the path first enters another cap's contact circle (BumperCircle.Radius: that cap's radius plus the ball's), when the ball already stands inside one (the caps of a wall overlap, and a ball in the notch between two of them bounces off the second at once), or when the kicked line does not clearly leave the cap just hit: a line within a hair of its tangent could re-enter it on a rounding error. Balloons are ignored: they pop, or the ball ricochets off them, on the way.

It stops. The bounce that takes the count past the chain target and every later one are mirror reflections, whose spread ends any exchange between mushrooms. The chain rule's cap on bounces (MushroomChainRules.MaxBouncesPerStretch) ends what is left.

The point of on where a ball coming from from must land for its mirror reflection to point at toward , with the offset the ball lands at (0 dead centre, 1 a graze) and the side of the mushroom it lands on (+1 left of the line from from to its centre, -1 right).

False when there is none: either end is on or inside the mushroom, or the straight way between them crosses it. Also false, never a wrong point, when rounding leaves the answer short of a true reflection, which an end a hair off the circle can do.

The point lies on the arc of the circle both ends see. Along that arc the surface normal turns steadily past the bisector of the two ways out of the point, from behind it at one end to ahead of it at the other, and the landing point is where they meet. So the arc brackets the one answer, and halving it finds that answer whenever there is one.
